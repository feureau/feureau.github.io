Saturated, Unsaturated & the Double Bond

An interactive look at why the shape of a fat molecule decides whether it is solid or liquid — and how it treats your LDL cholesterol.

Saturated fat
Every carbon in the chain is bonded to the maximum number of hydrogen atoms it can hold. There are no double bonds — the chain is "saturated" with hydrogen. Because the chain is straight, these molecules stack neatly, which is why saturated fats like butter, coconut oil and palm oil are solid or semi-solid at room temperature.

Cis vs trans
Both molecules have the same double bond and the same number of hydrogens — the difference is which side of the double bond the two chain halves sit on. In the cis form (left) both halves are on the same side, forcing a ~35° kink (dashed blue lines). In the trans form (right) the halves are on opposite sides, so the chain stays straight — which is why trans fats behave in the body almost exactly like saturated fats.
Why shape matters
Straight chains slide past each other and stack into a dense, ordered solid — that's butter, coconut oil and palm oil. Kinked cis chains get tangled and leave gaps, so they stay liquid — that's olive, canola, peanut and sunflower oil. The same geometry plays out in your body: straight (saturated / trans) fats tend to raise LDL cholesterol, while kinked unsaturated fats lower or don't raise it.
